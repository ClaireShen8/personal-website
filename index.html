Naval Research Enterprise Internship Program

This summer I had the opportunity to intern at both a software engineering lab (6 weeks) and a rapid prototyping lab (4 weeks). The below projects detail my primary work from the internship, on 2 separate project sprints.

Air-Gapped Desktop Video Annotation Application

I built an offline desktop app that turns multi-hour video into a training-ready dataset. Model-assisted pre-labeling speeds up annotation. Every image and label traces back to its source video and frame. Labeling now runs about 3x faster.

The Problem

A computer vision team was spending hours each week manually scrubbing long videos, isolating events, and labeling frames for model training. Because the data was sensitive, the entire workflow had to remain air-gapped, which made dataset organization and traceability especially difficult. Years of inconsistent folder structures and annotation formats had created a major bottleneck in producing reliable training datasets.

With four weeks left in my internship, I turned a 4 hour per week labeling assignment into a project sprint to build an offline application that streamlined the full video-labeling lifecycle.

What I Built

After interviewing the engineers who used the existing workflow, I built a Python desktop application with Qt and OpenCV for video review, event extraction, annotation, dataset management, and YOLO-ready export.

The application includes high-speed video scrubbing, progressive frame sampling, bounding-box and keypoint annotation, keypoint propagation, and GPU/CPU-aware pose-estimation pre-labeling. It also generates event-aware train/validation/test splits to prevent related frames from leaking across datasets and enforces a standardized local folder structure with a reviewable file-repair workflow.

I used agentic AI throughout development to implement detailed specifications and iterate rapidly, while manually designing the interface in Figma and validating the application with a suite of 600 unit and integration tests.

My Impact

Converted a tedious labeling task into a streamlined workflow, cutting a weekly 8 hour workflow to 2 across 100GB of video. Every image and label traces back to its source video and exact frame. I documented the tool's remaining limitations for whoever picks it up next. My recommendation for the next stage is a confidence-thresholded review loop, where the model annotates every frame and an analyst reviews only what it flags as uncertain.

Ingestion Pipeline for Unstructured PDFs

I scoped a 3 stage plan (ingestion, extraction, prediction) to automate numerical estimates from several hundred inconsistently formatted failure reports. In a 3 week sprint, I built the ingestion stage with Docling, converting PDFs into structured Markdown with tables and layout intact.

The Problem

A Navy rapid prototyping lab maintained hundreds of part malfunction reports. Their goal was to train a machine learning model to predict when a part will malfunction based on historical data. This would reduce the manual labor of analysts who read through these lengthy reports to produce estimates.

The problem: the data was unstructured and unstandardized. Documents contained a mix of tables, structured sections, and narrative text. The available fields varied between reports, and the location of information on each page could not be predicted.

Before automated extraction or prediction could be attempted, the documents needed to be converted into a consistent, machine-readable representation.

What I Built

I scoped the larger problem into 3 stages: ingestion, extraction, and prediction, then defined a 3 week sprint around the ingestion stage. I implemented a layout-aware pipeline using Docling to analyze each page, identify headings, paragraphs, and tables, apply OCR when necessary, and convert the reports into structured Markdown while preserving document organization and table information. I also researched and documented an architecture for the remaining extraction and prediction stages.

My Impact

Every report now reaches downstream stages in one consistent format. I handed off working ingestion software and a roadmap. Continuing the project requires validated extraction, historical outcome data, and analyst input on which variables drive their estimates.

Note: I was honored to place 3rd of 30 interns for the final technical presentation to base leadership!

DermLens

Teledermatology · Computer vision

Helped develop a computer-vision-enabled teledermatology platform by conducting user research, building a longitudinal dataset of more than 5,000 clinical images, and fine-tuning CNNs to classify treatment-site erythema.

Website Research Poster
The Problem

DermLens is designed to make follow-up for non-surgical skin cancer treatment more accessible. Patients undergoing topical treatment need their treatment sites monitored over time, traditionally requiring repeated in-person visits. The challenge is determining how photos and patient-reported symptoms collected remotely could provide physicians with useful information about treatment progression without replacing physician oversight.

What I Built

I have contributed across the project from its early user-research phase through computer vision development. I interviewed patients about their treatment experience and technology habits, helped translate the clinical workflow into application features and wireframes, and assisted dermatology clinic staff in assembling a custom longitudinal dataset of more than 5,000 treatment images. On the machine learning side, I fine-tuned and evaluated convolutional neural networks for classification of erythema, or treatment-site redness, as an initial step toward computer-assisted monitoring.

My Impact

My work helped move DermLens from a clinical idea toward a data-driven system that can be evaluated technically. I have helped connect patient and physician needs to the application's design while building the dataset and baseline computer vision capabilities needed for future longitudinal monitoring. The project has also allowed me to apply computer vision to a problem where model performance must ultimately be evaluated in the context of real clinical decisions.

User research & backend development In progress

HouseMeds

Web tool · Prescription price search

Performing user research and backend development for a household prescription-cost optimization platform that compares medication prices across pharmacies to identify lower-cost purchasing strategies.

Video Demo GitHub Repo
The Problem

Cash prices for the same prescription can vary substantially between pharmacies, and finding the lowest price becomes more complicated when optimizing medications for an entire household rather than for a single prescription. HouseMeds is designed to help families compare their medications together and determine a lower-cost purchasing strategy across pharmacies.

What I Built

I began by interviewing patients and physicians to understand how people currently purchase prescriptions and where the process creates friction. I assembled a five-person team to translate those findings into a full-stack web application. I am developing the backend using TypeScript and PostgreSQL, integrating pharmacy pricing through REST APIs, and working on the optimization logic that compares medication prices at the household level rather than treating every prescription independently.

My Impact

I have taken the project from an initial healthcare problem through user research, workflow definition, team formation, and early implementation. My role has focused on making sure the technical design reflects how patients actually manage medications while coordinating the work needed to turn those requirements into a functioning product. Because HouseMeds is still under active development, its impact so far is the creation of a user-informed architecture and development team rather than measured patient savings.

Computer Vision Vehicle Damage Assessment Device

SEAP · Science & Engineering Apprenticeship Program

Helped transform an open-ended AI concept into a real-time corrosion-detection system by building a custom dataset, fine-tuning a YOLO segmentation model, and deploying it on Raspberry Pi/Google Coral Edge TPU.

The Problem

Our intern team received an open-ended challenge: explore how AI could help Marines assess vehicle damage. There was no predefined user workflow, dataset, or technical approach. Any useful solution also needed to account for field constraints such as mobility, cost, durability, and limited computing resources.

What I Built

After helping narrow the problem to vehicle corrosion detection, our team collected, cleaned, and labeled more than 1,000 images of corrosion to create a custom dataset. We fine-tuned a YOLOv11 image-segmentation model to identify rust and tested the model using live camera input before adapting it for edge hardware including Raspberry Pi and Google Coral. Because the four-person intern team was initially researching many of the same problems independently, I also introduced daily standups and shared workspaces and established more regular communication with our supervising engineer.

My Impact

Our team turned a broad directive to "build an AI-enabled vehicle damage tool" into a working computer vision system capable of identifying corrosion in real time. I helped take the project through the complete applied machine learning workflow—from defining a practical problem and building our own dataset to model fine-tuning and constrained edge deployment—while improving collaboration between team members working in an unfamiliar technical domain.

Custom and Reverse-Engineered Parts for Marines

SEAP · Science & Engineering Apprenticeship Program

Designed, reverse-engineered, manufactured, and tested physical components for Marine end users using CAD and additive manufacturing, turning loosely defined requirements into deployable solutions.

The Problem

My first summer at the Navy's Rapid Prototyping Lab centered on practical engineering problems brought in by Marines and machinists. Some requirements were loosely defined, while other projects involved military vehicle components that were difficult to source through conventional replacement channels. The challenge was to understand what the end user actually needed and quickly turn that need into a manufacturable physical solution.

What I Built

I designed, manufactured, and tested a physical component from a machinist's high-level requirements, communicating directly with Marine end users as the design progressed and ultimately supporting its deployment on base. I also used Fusion 360 and SOLIDWORKS to reverse-engineer hard-to-source vehicle components and reproduce them using additive manufacturing.

My Impact

These projects gave Marine users practical physical solutions rather than designs that stopped at the prototype stage. By combining direct conversations with end users, CAD modeling, reverse engineering, fabrication, and iterative testing, I learned how to take an incomplete real-world requirement and carry it through to a part that could actually be used. That experience became the foundation for the user-centered engineering approach I carried into my later Navy and AI projects.
